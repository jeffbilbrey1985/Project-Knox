LOCAL SEO — LOMBARD, ILLINOIS

Local SEO in Lombard, IL, for a business Google doesn't know exists yet.

Ranking badly and being absent altogether are two different problems. Most new businesses have the second one, and it's a shorter job than it sounds.

(331) 291-7400

There's probably a listing for you already, and you've never seen it.

Google assembles listings out of whatever it can find — a supplier directory, a permit, a licence record, somebody adding a shop while staring at a map. So a version of your business may be sitting out there right now with a wrong closing time, your name spelled the way somebody guessed it and no photographs at all, answering questions on your behalf while you get on with the work.

Claiming it puts you in charge of what people are already being shown. Verification is usually a postcard, a call or a short video walk of the premises, depending on what Google asks of your category. It's the highest-value afternoon on this whole page, and until it's done the rest of the search work is pushing on a locked door.

When everything is missing at once, the order matters.

Owners starting from zero tend to do the interesting parts first and the load-bearing parts never. So there's a sequence, and we work through it in this order.

  • Week one: the profile claimed and verified, the primary category chosen deliberately, the name, address and number saying one identical thing everywhere
  • Week two: the site live, with a separate page for each service you want more of rather than a single list of nine
  • Week three: photographs of the real place, every hour including the awkward ones, and the questions people phone up to ask answered in writing
  • From then on: a request behind every finished job, every review answered, and one page written or rewritten each month

The first ten reviews are the hardest and the most valuable.

A profile with nothing under it reads as either brand new or quietly closed, and a stranger can't tell which. Ten honest reviews settle that question outright. After ten it stops being a milestone and becomes a habit that runs in the background.

So the asking gets built into the job instead of relying on you remembering during a rush. One message goes out behind each completed piece of work, the same wording for everybody, and nobody gets chased a second time. On Growth that runs by itself. Before then, do it by hand for two weeks and you'll see the effect for yourself.

Never buy them, never write them yourself, and never offer anything in exchange for one. It breaks Google's rules, customers can smell it from a distance, and a wall of perfect scores on a two-month-old business achieves the opposite of what you were hoping for.

Your name, address and number have to agree with each other.

Somewhere out there is an old suite number, a mobile you stopped answering, and a version of your name using an ampersand where the sign says the word. Google reads all of it while working out whether you're one business or three, and a business it can't pin down is one it shows less often.

Tidying that up is dull, invisible work with an actual finish line, which makes it rare in this field. It gets done once, thoroughly, and then guarded whenever something about you changes.

Write for what gets typed, not for what goes on a sign.

A sign carries your business name. A search carries the problem — somebody wants their gutters cleared, or a barber open on a Monday, or whatever the equivalent is in your trade at half past nine on a weeknight. Those two sets of words barely overlap, and only one of them is how new customers arrive.

So each service worth having more of gets a page to itself, written the way the thing is said out loud, with the town where it belongs and nowhere it doesn't. Underneath that sits structured data, spelling out your trade, your hours and the area you cover in a form a machine can read without interpreting a layout to get at it.

What we won't say, and what happens every week instead.

Nobody sells position on that map. Google doesn't take money for placement in local results, so a guarantee of the top three is a promise about something the person making it doesn't control. Anyone offering you one is counting on you not checking.

In its place, here is the trade — named work, on a rhythm, written down and reported back.

  • The profile pushed forward every month rather than touched only when something breaks
  • Requests going out behind finished work, and every review that arrives answered in your voice
  • A page added or rewritten as you learn which services people are really ringing about
  • Directories and listings kept saying one consistent thing about who you are and where you are
  • Page speed and dead links watched, since Google quietly stops showing a page that broke
  • A short written account each month of what we did and what it shifted, blank months included

The profile wakes up first. The website takes longer.

Filling in a profile that was empty can show results within a few weeks, since you're handing Google facts it wanted anyway. A site earning trust is slower work — three months is a fair point at which to start judging it, and a crowded trade takes longer than that.

Expect the map to move before the pages do, and treat anybody promising it inside a month as somebody who has never done it.

Lombard

The whole village shares a single ZIP code, 60148, which quietly removes one of the crude signals a search engine might otherwise use to tell one end of Lombard from the other. Distance from whoever is searching still counts, and you can't move the shop. Everything else is yours to change: whether the profile is claimed, whether it's complete, whether it's active, and whether a page exists that says clearly what you sell. The Lombard Area Chamber of Commerce and Industry can put you in a room with other owners. It can't put you in a set of results, and that part is mechanical.

Questions people actually ask

Straight answers.

There's a listing for my business on Google that I never made. What's that?

An unclaimed profile, built by Google out of public information. Anyone can suggest an edit to one, so the details drift and you never hear about it. You claim it rather than create a second one, since a duplicate splits your reviews and confuses the map. If duplicates already exist, closing them properly is part of the work.

I have no reviews at all. Where do the first ones come from?

From the customers you already served, asked once, in the hour while you're still fresh in mind. A short message with a direct link does most of it — people intend to leave one and then life happens, and the link is what turns intending into done. Ask everybody the same way and never filter who gets asked, because that last part is against the rules and it shows.

If the Google profile is doing the work, do I need the site as well?

The profile can carry you further than most people expect, and if you only ever do one thing, do that one. It has limits, though. It can't describe a service properly, it can't answer the four questions people ask before booking, and the list of links under the map is a race your profile isn't even entered in. The two feed each other, which is why they're in one plan rather than sold separately.

How long before we start showing up for anything?

Profile work can register in weeks. Pages take about three months to be worth judging, and longer where several established businesses are already competing for the same three pins. What you should see early is the map listing looking complete and active, which is what earns the first calls while the slower half is still building.
