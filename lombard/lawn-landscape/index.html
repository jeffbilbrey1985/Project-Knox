LAWN & LANDSCAPE — LOMBARD, ILLINOIS

Websites, snow contracts and off-season work for lawn and landscape crews in Lombard, IL.

January's money was agreed in September. By the time a forecast makes the news the routes are full, and the only thing left to sell is a very long night.

Snow is sold in September and delivered in January.

Every customer who signs in autumn is a customer who isn't ringing round in a panic in December, and every one who doesn't sign is a gap you'll fill at three in the morning with somebody you've never worked for. The selling window is short, it's early, and it opens while your crews are still cutting grass.

Which is why it can't depend on you having a free evening. The renewal goes out to last winter's list on a date you set, the new enquiries come in through a page written for winter rather than a paragraph tacked on to the mowing site, and the agreement gets read and returned online instead of being carried around in a truck for a fortnight.

Per push or seasonal is the entire conversation, so put it on the page.

Homeowners and property managers are weighing two different bets and most of them don't know the terms. One pays for each visit, one pays a flat amount for the winter and transfers the risk to you. Both are reasonable and both get chosen for different reasons.

Explaining that in writing does the arguing for you in advance. What depth brings the truck out. Whether salt is included or billed on its own. What happens in a winter with almost no snow, and what happens in one with far too much. Say it plainly and you'll have a shorter conversation in September and no conversation at all in March.

  • The trigger depth written down where anybody can read it
  • Salt and ice melt priced clearly, not left to be discovered on an invoice
  • What a visit includes — drive, walks, the space in front of the door
  • How a mild winter and a brutal one each get billed

At four in the morning, nobody calls twice.

The phone at that hour is a property manager who needs a lot cleared before staff arrive, and if it rings out she calls the next number on her list and you never learn it happened. You were asleep, which is entirely reasonable, and it cost you a contract anyway.

The answering bolt-on is $100 a month and it picks up at that hour, takes the address and the detail, and leaves it written down and waiting for you. Going the other way, one message to a route the night before — we're on tonight, expect us around five — stops thirty separate phone calls before they start.

Eight months a year you're not the snow guy, and they forget.

A customer who only ever sees you when it's white outside has no reason to think of you in April, even if you'd happily do the mulch, the cleanup and the cutting. That's not disloyalty. You simply never came up.

So the list works both directions. The people who take a seasonal contract hear from you in the spring about the beds and the first cut. The mowing customers hear from you in September about the driveway. It's the same handful of names, worked twice, and it's how a business stops having two separate customer bases that don't know they overlap.

Winter invoicing goes wrong in ways summer invoicing doesn't.

Per-push work generates a pile of small invoices in a fortnight, all of them for visits made in the dark, some of them disputed a month later by somebody who genuinely can't remember whether you came on the fourteenth.

Invoicing the same day, from the truck, with the date and the property on it, settles almost all of that before it starts. Seasonal contracts get billed on an even schedule so the money isn't all in one month, cards on file take care of the rest, and what's outstanding is a screen you can look at rather than a thing you worry about at the yard.

Lombard

Lilacia Park at 150 S. Park Avenue exists because Colonel William Plum left the village his garden in 1927, along with something close to two hundred varieties of lilac. It's a public park that grew out of one man's patience with plants that do nothing at all for most of the year. That's worth holding on to in a village nicknamed after a flower — people here notice ground that's been looked after, and they notice it in May on the strength of work done the previous autumn. Snow contracts run on exactly the same logic, signed in one season and judged in another.
